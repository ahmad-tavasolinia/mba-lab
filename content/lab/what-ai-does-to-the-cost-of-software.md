This one is less tied to a single course and more to independent reading at the intersection of AI, entrepreneurship, and strategy, cross-referenced against the strategy material covered earlier in the project.

## The central question

If building software stops being scarce — if a capable small team, or increasingly a single person, can produce what used to require a much larger engineering organization — what actually remains scarce? And does that remaining scarce thing still belong to the same companies that owned the previous scarce thing?

## Key ideas

A meaningful share of software's historical defensibility was never really about the underlying idea. It was about the cost, time, and specialized talent required to build and maintain it well. Many products that looked strategically clever were, underneath, simply expensive and slow to replicate — and that expense was doing a lot of quiet strategic work.

As AI-assisted development collapses that cost, the sources of defensibility have to migrate somewhere else: distribution, trust, proprietary data, regulatory position, and switching costs — assets that used to be secondary to the technical build and are now closer to the entire game.

There's also a second-order effect worth naming directly. Lower building costs don't just help incumbents build faster — they increase the number of credible competitors for any given idea, since the capital and technical barrier to attempting it drops for everyone simultaneously. Speed, which used to be a genuine advantage, starts to look more like a baseline requirement just to stay in the conversation.

## My synthesis

Tying this back to the earlier strategy work: if durable advantage comes from a system of reinforcing, hard-to-copy activities, and one entire category of "hard to copy" — the cost and difficulty of building the software itself — is disappearing, then the remaining sources of advantage have to work harder and matter more than they used to. This should, in principle, favor smaller and more entrepreneurial teams that were previously locked out by the sheer capital cost of building competitive software, though it isn't obvious yet whether the bottleneck simply moves to distribution and capital, where incumbents still hold real advantages.

## Connections

This is a direct continuation of the [competitive advantage](/mba-lab/what-creates-competitive-advantage) entry — the same framework, applied to a case where one major source of defensibility is being actively removed by a general-purpose technology, rather than by a single competitor's specific choice.

## Questions I still have

If technical execution genuinely stops being the bottleneck, does judgment about what's worth building become the entire game — and is that judgment something that can be taught, or only accumulated through practice? And a more skeptical question: does this shift actually favor small teams in the way it's often described, or does it simply relocate the bottleneck to a place — distribution, capital, trust — where large incumbents are still comfortably ahead?

## Final perspective

My working view is that the scarce resource in software is migrating from the ability to build it to the judgment about what's worth building, plus the means to get it in front of the right people. That's a real shift in who's positioned to win — but I'm not yet convinced it shifts as far in favor of small teams as the current enthusiasm suggests.
