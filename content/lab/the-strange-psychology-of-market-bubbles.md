Part of a course on financial markets that spent real time on behavioral finance rather than treating it as a footnote to efficient-market theory — which turned out to be the more useful framing.

## The central question

How can intelligent, well-informed people participate in a bubble that they can later describe, with total clarity, as having obviously been a bubble?

## Key ideas

The standard explanation reaches for irrationality — greed, herd behavior, cognitive bias. That explanation is not wrong, but it is incomplete, because it implies that a smarter or more disciplined investor would simply opt out. In practice, many of the smartest, most disciplined investors participate too, and not always by mistake.

A more useful idea treats a bubble as something that can emerge from **individually rational** behavior aggregated badly. If everyone else appears to know something you don't, and prices keep rewarding participation, staying out is not obviously smarter than joining — it's just a different bet, with its own cost. Robert Shiller's work on narrative economics adds another piece: asset prices are never purely a function of cash flow, because a valuation always requires a story about the future, and stories spread the way anything socially contagious spreads, independent of whether they're true.

A third, quieter idea does a lot of the explanatory work: being early and being wrong look identical in real time. A skeptic who correctly identifies a bubble two years before it pops experiences the same outcome, for two years, as a skeptic who is simply wrong. That symmetry is what makes rational skepticism personally expensive to hold onto during a bubble, regardless of whether it's ultimately correct.

## My synthesis

Putting this together, a bubble looks less like a failure of individual reasoning and more like a coordination problem organized around a shared story. The story does not need to be false to eventually break — it only needs to become less plausible to enough people at the same time. That reframes the ending of a bubble too: it is usually not new information about fundamentals that pops it, but a shift in how believable the existing story has become.

## Connections

This sits directly on top of the distinction explored in [risk vs. uncertainty](/mba-lab/risk-is-not-the-same-as-uncertainty): from inside a bubble, the situation is experienced as ordinary risk with a favorable distribution. It only reveals itself as genuine uncertainty — a situation nobody had actually modeled correctly — once the story breaks.

## Questions I still have

If a bubble can be entirely made of individually rational decisions, is there any reliable way to detect one while it's still inflating — or is "bubble" fundamentally a label that can only be applied in hindsight, once the story has already changed? And how much analytical work does the word "bubble" actually do, versus functioning mostly as a retroactive judgment?

## Final perspective

I no longer think of a bubble primarily as a failure of individual judgment. It looks more like a coordination problem around a shared, believable story — which is exactly why it can absorb genuinely smart, well-informed people without requiring a single one of them to be foolish.
