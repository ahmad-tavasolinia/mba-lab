This exploration emerged from studying competitive strategy through a set of university-level lectures on industry structure and firm positioning, alongside independent reading on strategic management.

## The central question

Why do some companies remain dominant for decades — Toyota in lean manufacturing, Southwest in low-cost air travel — while others, often with superior products, lose their advantage within a single cycle?

The standard answer is "execution." I don't think that's a real answer. It describes an outcome, not a mechanism.

## Key ideas

The first distinction worth making is between **positioning** — where a company chooses to compete — and **operational effectiveness** — how well it performs generic activities relative to competitors. The two get conflated constantly, especially in casual business writing, but they behave very differently under competition. Operational effectiveness is a moving target that diffuses across an industry as best practices spread. Positioning, when it is built from a genuinely different configuration of activities, does not diffuse the same way.

The second idea is about **fit**. A single clever choice is rarely defensible on its own, because a single choice can usually be copied. What is harder to copy is a whole system of choices that reinforce each other — pricing, hiring, logistics, and marketing that only make sense together. Copying one piece without the others often makes a competitor worse off, not better, which is what gives this kind of advantage its durability.

The third idea sits above the firm entirely: **industry structure**. Before any individual company's strategy is considered, the structure of an industry — the intensity of rivalry, the bargaining power of suppliers and buyers, the threat of substitutes — sets a ceiling on how profitable any participant can be. A brilliant strategy in a structurally brutal industry still tends to produce mediocre returns.

## My synthesis

Putting these together, competitive advantage starts to look less like a wall a company builds and more like a current it has to keep swimming against. It is not a fixed possession; it is the ongoing output of a relationship between a firm's activities, each other, and an environment that keeps shifting. That reframes the right question. It is not "does this company have an advantage" but "what specifically makes this configuration of activities hard to copy, and for how long."

It also reframes what a strategist is actually doing. Less picking a clever tactic, more designing a system of trade-offs deliberate enough that a competitor copying any single piece of it would be copying the wrong thing.

## Connections

This links directly into questions about organizational design — a system of reinforcing activities is, in practice, an organizational structure, which is where the [reorganizations](/mba-lab/why-most-reorganizations-fail) entry picks up. It also raises an obvious tension with what's happening in software, explored in the [AI and the cost of software](/mba-lab/what-ai-does-to-the-cost-of-software) entry: if positioning advantage depends partly on the cost and difficulty of building certain capabilities, what happens when that cost collapses for everyone at once?

## Questions I still have

What happens to positioning-based advantage in industries where AI is collapsing the cost of building and distributing software — the exact kind of cost that used to make a configuration of activities hard to copy?

And a more uncomfortable one: is "fit between activities" actually a causal source of advantage, or is it a story we tell afterward about firms that happened to succeed for other reasons, including luck? I don't yet have a satisfying answer.

## Final perspective

My current view is that competitive advantage is closer to a current than a wall — something that has to be actively maintained through an interlocking set of choices, each individually unremarkable, collectively difficult to copy. The interesting strategic work is not finding the one clever move, but designing which trade-offs to make together, and being honest about how long the resulting advantage can realistically hold.
