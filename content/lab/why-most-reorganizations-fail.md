This entry grew out of a course on organizational behavior that spent a full unit on why structural interventions so often underperform expectations, paired with several real-world cases of large-company reorganizations.

## The central question

If restructuring so rarely delivers what it promises, why do organizations keep reaching for it as a first response to underperformance?

## Key ideas

An org chart captures formal reporting lines. It says almost nothing about how coordination actually happens day to day, which runs mostly through informal networks, trust, and shared context that a chart cannot represent. A reorganization can rearrange the formal lines while leaving the informal network almost entirely intact — or, worse, sever exactly the informal relationships that were doing the real coordinating work.

There is also a plain incentive story here worth being honest about. Restructuring is visible, fast to announce, and clearly attributable to leadership. Fixing incentive structures or shifting culture is slower, less visible, and harder to take credit for. That asymmetry pushes leaders toward the structural lever even when it isn't the right one for the actual problem.

A more structural idea, drawn from organizational design theory, is that every structure trades one coordination problem for a different one. Centralizing improves consistency and slows responsiveness. Decentralizing improves speed and weakens consistency. There is no structure without a corresponding weakness — which means restructuring rarely solves a problem outright, it relocates it.

## My synthesis

Putting these together, a lot of restructuring functions less as a genuine fix and more as a visible, legible action that substitutes for solving a harder, less visible problem — usually one involving incentives, trust, or culture. The org chart gets redrawn because redrawing it is something leadership can actually do quickly, not necessarily because the chart was the problem.

## Connections

This connects directly to the [competitive advantage](/mba-lab/what-creates-competitive-advantage) entry: a durable advantage is often described as a system of reinforcing activities, and an organizational structure is one of the clearest examples of such a system. Restructuring without understanding which informal relationships are load-bearing risks dismantling exactly the reinforcing fit that created the advantage in the first place.

## Questions I still have

Is there a reliable way to diagnose, before restructuring, whether a problem is genuinely structural — versus an incentive or culture problem simply wearing a structural disguise? And why does the appeal of restructuring survive so many publicly visible failures, across so many companies, without the pattern seeming to update anyone's priors?

## Final perspective

My current view is that restructuring is usually the most visible response available to leadership, not the most effective one — and the actual failure mode is mistaking visibility for effectiveness.
